KDI 572

Bendix/King DME Indicator
Modern Variant Edition
Published by

This guide covers the Bendix/King KDI 572 distance measuring equipment as fitted to the DC-3 Modern variant, a standalone companion to the main DC-3 Aircraft Operating Manual. The KDI 572 is the head that shows how far you are from a station, how fast you are closing on it, and how long until you get there. The Modern panel carries two, one per pilot, each channeling from the NAV radios and each with the HOLD trick that keeps the DME locked to a station while you tune an ILS. All of it is modeled, including slant range, the search behavior, and the signal memory.

1. Overview

KDI 572 at a glance

Type: DME indicator (master for a KN 63 transceiver)

Reads: Distance, groundspeed, time-to-station

Distance: Slant range, to 389 nm

Channeling: From NAV 1, NAV 2, or HOLD

Control: One four-position selector

Units fitted: Two (pilot and copilot)

Power: Function selector + DME breaker, DC Radio Bus

Distance measuring equipment answers a question a bearing cannot: not just which way to the station, but how far. The airborne set sends a pulse pair to a ground beacon, the beacon answers, and the box times the round trip and turns it into a distance. Pair that distance with a VOR radial and you have a fix from a single station; read how the distance changes and you also get your groundspeed and your time to the station, all three at once.

The KDI 572 is the cockpit indicator, the master unit, for a KN 63 remote DME transceiver tucked away in the airframe. It does not have its own tuning; instead it channels from one of the NAV radios, so whatever VOR, VORTAC, or ILS/DME you tune on the NAV side, the DME follows. On this aircraft, two of them are fitted, one for each pilot, and they work independently. A DC-3 kept current for instrument work would carry exactly this: a simple, reliable distance readout slaved to the navigation radios.

Power: the selector is the switch

Each KDI 572 is turned on by rotating its function selector off OFF. Beyond that it needs avionics power on the DC Radio Bus and its own DME circuit breaker closed (the two units are on separate breakers). A dark indicator usually means the selector is in OFF; then check the breaker and the avionics master.

4. Controls in Detail

The function selector

One knob runs the whole unit. It is a four-position rotary with stops at each end, and it steps OFF, N1, HLD, N2 from left to right:

  • OFF turns the unit off and the display dark.
  • N1 channels the DME from the NAV 1 radio.
  • N2 channels the DME from the NAV 2 radio.
  • HLD holds the DME on whatever it was last channeled to, even when you retune the NAV radios.

Because the positions are in a fixed sequence, HOLD sits between N1 and N2: you reach it by turning toward HOLD from whichever source you want to keep. Coming from N1 it holds NAV 1 and shows 1H; coming from N2 it holds NAV 2 and shows H2. The channeling section below walks through why that matters.

5. Reading the Display

Three numbers, shown together: distance on the left in nautical miles, groundspeed in the middle in knots, time-to-station on the right in minutes. Until the unit locks onto a station, all three show dashes.

A short memory

If the signal drops out for a moment, the readout coasts on its last value for a few seconds rather than flashing straight to dashes. This keeps the numbers steady through brief interruptions; if the signal is truly gone, the windows revert to dashes after the memory expires.

6. The Function Selector and Channeling

The KDI 572 has no frequency of its own. It borrows one from a NAV radio, and the selector decides which.

N1 and N2

In N1 the DME channels from the NAV 1 radio; in N2 it channels from NAV 2. On the Modern panel, NAV 1 is the GNS 530's VLOC side and NAV 2 is the KX 165A. Whenever you retune the selected NAV radio, the DME retunes itself immediately to match, so you simply tune the VOR or VORTAC you want on the NAV side and pick the matching source here.

HOLD, and why it exists

HOLD is the clever part. Put the selector in HLD and the DME freezes on whatever station it was last channeled to, ignoring any later changes to the NAV radios. The annunciation tells you which source is frozen: 1H for NAV 1, H2 for NAV 2.

This earns its keep on an instrument approach. Many ILS frequencies have no DME of their own, and on an approach you may need both NAV radios tuned to the ILS. So before you start down, tune a nearby VORTAC, channel it on the DME, then switch to HOLD. Now you can retune both NAV radios to the ILS and the DME keeps reading distance from the VORTAC right through the approach.

HOLD does not follow your tuning

The flip side of that convenience: while the selector is in HOLD, retuning the NAV radios does nothing to the DME. If you forget you left it in HOLD, you can find yourself reading distance from a station you tuned ten minutes ago. The 1H or H2 annunciation is there precisely to remind you. Back out to N1 or N2 when you want the DME to follow your tuning again.

7. Operating Procedures

Power up and channel a station

  1. With avionics power on and the DME breaker closed, rotate the function selector off OFF to N1 or N2, whichever NAV radio carries the station you want.
  2. Tune the VOR or VORTAC on that NAV radio. The DME searches briefly (dashes), then locks on and shows the distance, followed by groundspeed and time.
  3. Identify the station by its Morse code on the NAV audio, just as you would for navigation. The DME has no separate identifier of its own.

Keep DME through an ILS approach

  1. Before the approach, tune a nearby VORTAC on a NAV radio and channel it on the DME (N1 or N2).
  2. Confirm a good lock and the right station.
  3. Turn the selector to HLD. Confirm 1H or H2.
  4. Retune the NAV radios to the ILS. The DME keeps reading from the held VORTAC throughout.
  5. After the approach, return the selector to N1 or N2 so the DME follows your tuning again.

8. Slant Range, Groundspeed, and Time

Slant range, not ground distance

The distance a DME shows is slant range, the straight-line distance from your aircraft to the station, not the distance across the ground. The two are close when you are far away and low, and they diverge as you get near and high. Directly over the station, the DME cannot read zero; it reads your height above it, roughly one nautical mile for every 6000 feet. A handy rule: once your distance is at least three times your altitude, the difference between slant range and ground distance is negligible.

Groundspeed and time-to-station

The DME computes groundspeed from how fast the slant range is changing, and time-to-station by dividing the distance by that speed. Both numbers are only honest when you are tracking more or less directly to or from the station. Fly a track that passes to one side of it and the rate of change no longer matches your true groundspeed: the groundspeed reads low, falling toward zero on an arc or abeam the station, and the time-to-station, computed from it, drops out to dashes once it is no longer meaningful. That is not a fault, it is geometry. Trust groundspeed and time only when the station is roughly ahead of you or behind you.

9. Popping the Unit Out

Each KDI 572 can be opened in its own floating window, handy on a second monitor, in VR, or for a closer read of the numbers. Because there are two units, there are two commands in X-Plane's keyboard and joystick settings: "Toggle KDI 572 DME 1 (pilot) popup" and "Toggle KDI 572 DME 2 (copilot) popup". Bind whichever you use to a key or button; press to open, press again to close.

Inside the window the selector works as it does in the cockpit: roll the mouse wheel over it, or click its halves, to step through OFF, N1, HLD, and N2. Click the unit's nameplate to dock or undock the window. Docked, it fills the window edge to edge; undocked, it floats as an ordinary window you can drag and resize, with the bezel letterboxed so the face keeps its proportions.

10. Normal and Abnormal Indications

Healthy operation

A working KDI 572 channeled to a station you are tracking shows all three numbers steadily: distance counting down as you close, a groundspeed near your true groundspeed, and a sensible time-to-station. No dashes, and no unexpected 1H or H2.

What to watch for

IndicationMeaningWhat to do
Display darkThe selector is in OFF, the DME breaker is open, or there is no avionics power.Turn the selector off OFF, then check the breaker and the avionics master.
Persistent dashesNo DME signal: out of range, terrain in the way, the NAV is tuned to a station with no DME, or below line of sight.Confirm the NAV is tuned to a DME-equipped station and that you are within range and line of sight.
Time-to-station dashed, groundspeed lowYou are not tracking toward or away from the station.Normal. Both read true again when the station is ahead of or behind you.

DME range is terrain-aware

Reception in the DC-3 is modeled with real propagation. DME is line-of-sight, so range grows with altitude and a station can drop out behind high terrain even when the chart says you are close enough. A lock that comes and goes near terrain is the model working, not a failure.

11. Notes and Tips

  • The DME follows the NAV radio. There is nothing to tune on the box. Tune the station on NAV 1 or NAV 2 and select the matching source.
  • Identify on the NAV audio. The DME has no identifier of its own. Confirm the station by its Morse code on the NAV side.
  • HOLD for the approach. Channel a VORTAC, switch to HOLD, then tune the ILS, and the DME stays with the VORTAC. Watch for 1H or H2 so you remember it is held.
  • It is slant range. Near and high, the distance is more than the ground distance, and it will not read zero overhead. Past three times your altitude, the difference is nothing.
  • Groundspeed needs the station ahead or behind. Off to the side, the groundspeed sags toward zero and the time-to-station dashes out. That is geometry, not a fault.
  • Two boxes, two breakers. The captain's and first officer's DMEs power up and channel independently.
