Fuel Icing System NEW

Three distinct cold weather fuel phenomena modeled:

1. Carburetor Icing (most dangerous)

  • Physics-based temperature modeling:
    • Modelled on NACA's 1944 laboratory icing tests of this carburetor on this engine (ARR E4J18, the Bendix-Stromberg PD-12F5 on the R-1830-C4): throttle ice in clear humid air, rain and cloud ice on the spray bar and impeller vanes; a pressure-injection carburetor has no venturi refrigeration
    • Throttle position decides it: condensate from the expansion through the venturis freezes on plates chilled by the throttle expansion, so clear-air ice needs part throttle, in cruise or on a low-power letdown; with the throttles wide open NACA could make none at any temperature
    • Clear-air icing up to about 13°C carburetor air in saturated air and 19°C when drier, and slow: a few percent of air flow per quarter hour
  • Environmental factors:
    • Dew point, pressure, cloud water and rain sampled at the aircraft's own altitude, not at sea level
    • Rain or cloud below about 18°C ices the induction system fast at any power, the case the crews' preheat-fifteen-minutes-before rule was written for: heavy rain at 4°C halved NACA's air flow in a quarter of an hour
  • Formation and melting follow NACA's enthalpy line for this carburetor: preheat held at 32°C sits above it at any humidity and clears the ice in the half minute the manual's procedure allows; the Hangar caution is computed from the same rate
  • Carburetor heat raises the induction air by the documented 56 to 60°C at full heat; the lever modulates and locks anywhere, and the power penalty follows it
  • Carburetor Air Temperature gauge: Reads the bulb in the air scoop above the carburetor, so it shows the preheat rise; hold 32°C, 38°C maximum with preheat
  • Carburetor ice costs real power: the plugin's ice level drives X-Plane's own carburetor ice every frame, so the loss the gauge and the STATUS tab warn of is the one the engine delivers

2. Fuel Line Icing

  • Water in each tank's sump, in litres: condensation while parked, the cold-soaked landing, dissolved water separating as the fuel cools, and what each fuel delivery brings; kept in LES.ini
  • Water reaches the strainer bowl when a sump overflows or sloshing stirs it to the outlet; above freezing a slug past the bowl stumbles the engine, below freezing it freezes onto the screen
  • The iced strainer drops fuel pressure by up to 8 PSI and, blocked, starves the engine; a frozen sump cannot be drained until the fuel is warm
  • Sump each tank and drain each engine's strainer bowl from the STATUS tab, on the ground, the drains the aeroplane has

3. Cold Start Vaporization

  • Fuel temperature effects on vaporization
  • Extended electric primer activation required in cold (hold primer switch longer)
  • Startup roughness based on fuel/engine temperature

Cabin Combustion Heater System NEW

Full simulation of the Stewart-Warner (Janitrol) S-100 combustion heater per T.O. 1C-47-2 Section IV. Burns aviation gasoline from the right main tank in a sealed combustion chamber, with a heat exchanger transferring heat to cabin air via squirrel-cage blower.

State Machine (9 States)

  • OFF: Master switch off, no power, or prerequisites not met
  • PRE_IGNITION: Fuel pump priming, solenoids opening (~1 sec)
  • IGNITION: Spark plug firing, waiting for combustion (cold weather extends)
  • IGNITION_FAILED: Failed to light, 3-second pause before automatic retry
  • COMBUSTION: Fuel burning, discharge duct temp rising toward 130°C
  • CYCLING_OFF: Fenwal thermostat opened at 130°C, fuel cut, coasting down
  • CYCLING_ON: Thermostat closed at 120°C, re-igniting (faster than initial start)
  • OVERHEAT: Discharge duct temp hit 176°C (350°F), drop-out switch activating
  • DROPOUT_LATCHED: Drop-out fuse blown, heater locked out until maintenance reset

Thermal Model

  • Combustion chamber temp: Internal, ramps toward ~450°C during combustion, decays toward ambient when off
  • Discharge duct temp: shown in the DC3 Hangar, lags behind chamber due to heat exchanger thermal mass. Fenwal switches sense this temperature. Normal cycling range 120-130°C
  • Outlet temp: Cabin delivery temperature, further reduced by heat exchanger efficiency (max ~80°C)
  • Blower speed affects heat transfer rate through the exchanger
  • Cold ambient temperatures slow warm-up (up to 3x slower below -30°C)

Cold Weather Ignition

  • Ignition attempts required increase as outside air temperature falls (Standard and Veteran; disabled on Tourist)
  • Above 0°C: guaranteed ignition on first attempt
  • 0°C to -20°C: success rate decreases linearly (100% to 30% per attempt)
  • Below -20°C: 30% per attempt with extended ignition time (6 seconds)
  • Cumulative probability across retries (up to 5 attempts)

Fuel Consumption

  • Consumes avgas from right main tank (0.8 GPH low blower, 1.5 GPH high blower)
  • Fuel starvation: heater extinguishes if tank runs empty
  • Fuel solenoid stuck open failure: leaks ~0.15 GPH even when heater is off

Ground Blower Interlock

  • On ground, ram air unavailable for combustion
  • Ground blower requires HEATER_GROUND_CTRL CB + both engines above 1200 RPM (or GPU connected)
  • In flight, ram air supplies combustion airflow automatically

Overheat Protection

  • Fenwal drop-out switch: One-shot thermal fuse at 176°C (350°F)
  • Permanently disables heater until maintenance reset (ground only)
  • Maintenance reset required, on the ground only: REPLACE FUSE on the MAINTENANCE tab, or the reset command

Failure Modes (8 Types)

  • Thermostat stuck closed: No cycle-off, temperature rises past 130°C toward dropout
  • Thermostat stuck open: Won't re-light after cycling off, temperature decays to ambient
  • Ignition failure: Spark plug dead, heater cannot light
  • Fuel pump failure: No fuel pressure, flame extinguishes or can't start
  • Fuel solenoid stuck open: Fuel leak when heater off (fire hazard)
  • CO contamination: Heat exchanger crack leaks carbon monoxide into cabin air
  • Compartment fire: Heater compartment fire (cascading failure, or scheduled directly)
  • Drop-out switch failure: No overheat protection (safety device bypass)
  • Cascading: Thermostat stuck closed + dropout switch failure leads to runaway overheat and compartment fire
  • All failures use MTBF-based random triggering and scheduled failure modes

Carbon Monoxide

  • CO builds up gradually when heat exchanger crack failure is active during combustion
  • Cabin CO concentration is published in parts per million
  • Dissipates whenever combustion stops

Fire Extinguisher Integration

  • Heater compartment fire triggers cabin fire warning light
  • Fire extinguisher selector valve has HEATER COMPT position
  • CO₂ discharge to heater compartment suppresses fire
  • Heater remains locked out (DROPOUT_LATCHED) after fire suppression

Electrical Load

  • Blower motor: 3A (low) / 6A (high) on HEATER_CONTROL CB
  • Ignition unit: 2A during ignition and cycling-on states
  • Fuel pump: 1.5A (Adel 21990)
  • Fuel solenoids: 0.5A when open
  • Ground blower motor: 45A on HEATER_GROUND_CTRL CB

Hydraulic Cowl Flap System REWRITE

Complete rewrite based on Quebecair DC-3 General Arrangement Part 3, paragraphs 40-44. Replaces the previous electric-motor model with the authentic hydraulic four-way control valve described in the Quebecair AOM. Cross-checked against T.O. 1C-47-2 Para 3-22 (Hydraulic System Applications list, which explicitly names cowl flaps as a hydraulic consumer).

Architecture

  • Two completely independent hydraulic circuits, one per engine, fed from the brake pressure line with returns to the brake return line
  • Four-way ball-type control valves on the right side wall adjacent to the First Officer's seat
  • Double-acting chrome-moly strut mounted on the lower segment of the anti-drag ring, piston rod connected to the cowl flap control tie rods
  • Cowl flap actuator overpressure is covered by the thermal relief system, which models a relief valve on the cowl flap circuits against thermal expansion in the nacelle
  • No electrical involvement at all: the previous COWL_FLAPS_L and COWL_FLAPS_R circuit breakers have been removed from the secondary bus panel, along with the CowlFlapMotor electrical stub

Four-Position Detented Selector Valve

  • OFF - All valve ports blocked. Fluid is trapped in both ends of the strut, hydraulically locking the piston. The flap cannot move regardless of hydraulic pressure, engine state, or aerodynamic load.
  • CLOSE - Fluid drives the strut toward closed. Pressure-gated and speed-scaled.
  • TRAIL - Both ends of the strut connected together. Hydraulic resistance is removed and the flap floats to aerodynamic equilibrium, typically 10-30% open depending on indicated airspeed. On the ground (below 5 kts) with no airflow, the flap holds its current position.
  • OPEN - Fluid drives the strut toward full open. Pressure-gated and speed-scaled.

Hydraulic Pressure Dependency

  • Minimum pressure for flap movement: 400 PSI (estimated; manual is silent on this threshold but it sits between accumulator pre-charge 250 PSI and regulator cut-in 650 PSI)
  • Rated speed at regulator cut-out (875 PSI): approximately 8 seconds full travel, linearly scaled for partial pressure
  • With both engines running and the main system at normal operating pressure, flaps respond immediately
  • With engines stopped and the system depleted, the pilot must stroke the hand pump to pre-charge the accumulator before the flaps will move. Each full cycle draws pressure from the accumulator. This matches the procedure shown in period instruction films
  • Pressure draw while driving: ~3 PSI/sec from the main system (estimated, matches wing flap consumer pattern)

Actual cowl flap position, the detented valve selector position and the rotary knob animation are published per engine. Both the selector state and the flap position persist across flights.

Operating Notes

  • OFF is a true hydraulic lock, not a "motor idle" position. A flap set at half-open with the selector in OFF stays at half-open even if hydraulic pressure is lost afterward
  • TRAIL is aerodynamic float, not a fixed intermediate position. Previous electric motor code treated TRAIL as a 35% target; the real valve simply connects both strut chambers and lets the flap find its own balance
  • The engine cover interlock continues to block the TRAIL and OPEN positions while an engine cover is installed, allowing only OFF and CLOSE
  • Valve selector state and actual flap position both persist in LES.ini across flights

Known Limitations

  • The Quebecair manual is silent on minimum actuation pressure, nominal travel time at rated pressure, stroke volume, and exact TRAIL aerodynamic equilibrium curve. The values used (400 PSI min, 8 sec at rated, linear speed scaling, IAS-driven TRAIL target) are engineering estimates documented inline in the source code and may be refined if primary-source data becomes available

Wheel Brakes and Emergency Air System NEW

The wheel brakes now answer to pressure. Per MM para 2-416 to 2-439 there are three ways to work them: main hydraulic pressure through the power brake control valve on the rudder pedals, the hydraulic hand pump, and the emergency air system, which drives the same brakes with compressed air when the hydraulic pressure is gone. It is a wheel brake, not an airbrake.

  • Pedal braking fades with the hydraulics. With both engine-driven pumps stopped the pedals weaken over a minute or two as the system bleeds down, and below about 100 PSI the linings do not bite at all. Strokes on the hand pump buy some of it back
  • Shuttle valve at each brake assembly passes whichever pressure is higher, hydraulic or air, and closes the other port. Same discs, same pistons, different working fluid, so the two systems cannot fight each other
  • The air bottle holds 1,590 PSI, charged on the ground. There is no compressor aboard. The air pressure control valve feeds both wheels together
  • No pressure reducer, so the valve meters bottle pressure straight to the brakes. The first application is the strongest the brakes will ever see, and every one after it is weaker as the bottle falls
  • The pedals do nothing on air. The shuttle valve has closed their port, so there is no differential braking while the emergency system is in use
  • The bottle is spent per application, not per second held. Each opening of the valve draws about twelve percent of the charge and holding it costs nothing more, so pumping the lever is what empties it. Roughly twenty applications before there is nothing left
  • The control valve lever and its 0 to 2,000 PSI gauge are not modelled in 3D yet. On the real aircraft the lever sits left of the centreline just below the windshield Vee. Until it is built the valve is on three commands for a keybind: les/dc3/cmd/brakes/emerg_air_open, _close and _toggle
  • The charge is kept between flights. RECHARGE sits beside FILL UP on the MAINTENANCE tab of the DC3 Hangar, on the ground only. Tourist keeps the bottle full at all times
  • X-Plane's own keyboard brake commands are intercepted. The hold commands become pedal demand through the same pressure model, and the two toggle commands become the parking brake, gated on pressure exactly as the knob is, so they no longer pull the knob out past its own gate
  • Differential braking for taxi assist is pressure-gated on the same terms. Wheel chocks still hold with no pressure at all, because a chock is a block and not a brake
  • The STATUS tab counts the applications left, at full braking and before the bottle is empty, rather than reading out a pressure alone. Its amber warning sits at 875 PSI, the pressure below which an application no longer brakes as hard as a full pedal would, rather than at a round number. On Tourist it says the bottle does not empty

Brake Lining Wear

The DC-3 brake is a single-disc self-adjusting spot brake (MM para 2-418). As the linings wear, the return spring plates draw the adjusting pin through its packing nut and the piston walks in to meet the disc, so clearance and pedal position never change. Two things follow from that, and the second is the one worth knowing.

  • Braking stays at full strength for the whole life of the lining. No soft pedal, no long travel, no cue of any kind in the cockpit. The manual has it that the brake requires no adjustment during the entire life of the lining
  • So the only place the wear shows is the MAINTENANCE tab, which gives percent remaining per wheel with the adjusting-pin recession in thousandths beside it, the manual's own gauge for the job
  • The limit is a quarter inch of pin recession. At that point the manual allows 25 more landings and no more, and the tab counts them down in amber. Past zero the linings are through and that wheel keeps only a quarter of its braking, enough to pull the aeroplane toward the good side
  • Wear follows energy rather than hours. A heavy landing braked hard costs more than a light one, riding the brakes through a long taxi costs more again, and braking asymmetrically wears the two wheels apart
  • RELINE BRAKES on the MAINTENANCE tab, on the ground, restores both wheels and clears the deadline
  • Tourist accrues no lining wear at all. Veteran wears the linings faster

Brake Overheating

  • Overheating a brake circuit past its thermal relief valve used to cost nothing. It now leaves that wheel permanently weaker, holding about 60 percent of its authority, until the brakes are relined, and the MAINTENANCE tab names the wheel and the reason

Brake Failures

  • Shuttle valve stuck, per wheel. The poppet sticks over the hydraulic port, so that wheel loses its normal braking while emergency air still reaches it: a failure in the backup system that disables the primary. You meet it on an ordinary landing when the aeroplane pulls to one side, and opening the emergency air valve brakes both wheels together again
  • Brake line leak, per wheel. That wheel loses hydraulic braking, and fluid escapes whenever the pedal is down, draining the reservoir with it. FILL UP restores the fluid; repairing the failure stops the loss
  • Power brake control valve (MM para 2-422), the metering valve on the pedals. Failed, neither wheel has hydraulic braking, which is precisely the case the emergency air system was fitted for
  • Emergency air bottle leak. The bottle or a fitting weeps, and this one runs on calendar time rather than flight hours: roughly 250 PSI gone after a day parked and half the charge after four. A healthy bottle loses nothing at all, which is the manual's own acceptance test (para 2-436, no leakage in a 24-hour period)

CUSTOM ICING PHYSICS ENGINE

Messinger Heat Balance Model NEW

Complete replacement of X-Plane's built-in icing with a physics-driven custom model based on the Messinger heat balance equation (1953). The engine computes ice accretion from first principles using thermodynamic energy balance at each surface.

  • 12 independent ice zones tracked: wing leading edges (L/R), horizontal stabilizer, vertical stabilizer, propeller blades (L/R), pitot tubes (L/R), windshield, antenna, and cowl inlets (L/R)
  • Per-zone energy balance: kinetic and aerodynamic heating vs convective, evaporative, and sensible cooling
  • Freezing fraction determines ice type: rime (opaque, rough, sheds well), glaze (clear, smooth, adhesive, hard to shed), or mixed. The type follows the mass-weighted fraction of the ice that has accreted, so old rime stays rime when the conditions dip toward glaze and a glaze skin on a rime slab reads mixed
  • Collection efficiency computed from Stokes number and body geometry; small bodies (pitot) collect ice faster than large surfaces (wing)
  • Convective heat transfer via Reynolds/Nusselt with Sutherland viscosity correction and laminar/turbulent flow regimes
  • Churchill-Bernstein correlation for cylindrical bodies (pitot, antenna)
  • Surface skin temperature model with per-zone thermal time constants
  • Natural sublimation when not in active icing conditions

Weather-Driven LWC/MVD Estimation NEW

Since X-Plane does not expose Liquid Water Content or Mean Volume Diameter directly, the engine estimates them from available weather data:

  • Cloud layer analysis from XPLMGetWeatherAtLocation() determines in-cloud conditions
  • Cloud character taken from X-Plane's reported cloud layer type (cirrus, scattered, broken, overcast, stratus); coverage, cloud depth and temperature then scale the resulting water content
  • LWC estimated from the reported cloud layer: up to 0.25 g/m³, scaled by coverage, by position within the layer, and reduced below -20°C
  • MVD estimated from the reported cloud layer: 10 μm (high/scattered), 17-18 μm (stratiform), 20 μm (broken)
  • Precipitation augments LWC; freezing rain/drizzle gives large MVD (100-500 μm, SLD conditions)
  • Icing severity classification: none, trace, light, moderate, severe

De-Ice Boot Integration NEW

The icing engine integrates with the existing pneumatic de-ice boot system for realistic ice shedding:

  • Boot shedding effectiveness depends on ice thickness: boots are ineffective below 3 mm (ice too thin to crack), optimal at 6-25 mm
  • Ice type matters: rime ice sheds well (1.0x effectiveness), mixed ice sheds moderately (0.75x), glaze ice sheds poorly (0.45x due to adhesion)
  • Inflation pressure must exceed 6 PSI for effective shedding (linear ramp from 4-8 PSI)
  • Intercycle residual ice: boots never shed 100% of ice; residual ice accumulates between cycles
  • Very thick ice (>25 mm) is harder to shed, with decreasing effectiveness
  • Critical realism: pilot must wait for sufficient buildup before cycling boots, matching real DC-3 procedures

Propeller Alcohol Anti-Ice Integration NEW

  • Queries existing prop anti-ice alcohol pump state
  • Effective ice prevention above -15°C OAT; partial protection below
  • RPM affects alcohol distribution (centrifugal force at blade roots)
  • Empty alcohol tank provides no protection

Pitot Heat Model NEW

  • 162W per pitot tube heating element (5.77A at 28V per AN 01-40NK-2)
  • Sufficient to prevent icing under normal conditions
  • Sufficient to prevent pitot icing whenever the element is powered; loss of the pitot requires an element failure or a pulled breaker
  • Requires electrical power and functioning circuit breakers

Windshield and Ground Frost NEW

  • Windshield frost and ice are modelled separately, from glass temperature, defrost heat and the glass's own thermal mass
  • Wipers slowly clear windshield frost, but only while hydraulic pressure is up, the blades are not frozen to the glass, and ice has not built up past a thin layer; they never clear ice
  • Ground frost forms on parked aircraft when OAT is below 0°C and surface temperature is below dewpoint
  • Frost persists across sessions via state persistence
  • X-Plane handles windshield frost/ice visual rendering

Aerodynamic Penalties NEW

  • Wing ice reaches X-Plane's airframe ice weighted by its type: glaze at full weight, mixed at three quarters, rime and frost at half, so 25 mm of glaze is severe and 25 mm of rime half that. FLIGHT DECK OPERATIONS AND CREW WORKFLOW

Difficulty Modes NEW

The 2.0 Lua build had no difficulty concept: failure behaviour was a single global MTBF value. The C++ plugin introduces three presets that scale failure probability, abuse tolerance, wear accumulation and several engine-handling behaviours. The mode is saved in the [DIFFICULTY] section of LES.ini and is recorded with every logbook entry.

  • Tourist: random failures forced off, 5x abuse tolerance, quarter wear rate, primer auto-cutoff after 0.5 s per press, cylinder fuel charge capped at over-rich so the engine can never be fully flooded, levers immune to vibration drift
  • Standard: normal rates throughout, user controls the random failure toggle, primer auto-cutoff retained, flooding still capped, engine vibration drifts loose levers
  • Veteran: random failures forced on, failures twice as likely, abuse thresholds halved, double wear rate, checklist enforcement warnings, no primer auto-cutoff (continuous fuel flow, exactly as the real switch behaves) and no flooding cap, so a mishandled primer will load the cylinders and refuse to light
  • Other systems query the active mode directly: icing rate and boot effectiveness, hydraulic abuse limits, engine abuse timers and wear accumulation all read from it

Checklist System with Live Auto-Detection NEW

Version 2.0 carried the checklists as plain text tables in LES_24.lua with a manual tick box per line. The C++ checklist reads the aircraft state and tells you whether each item is actually satisfied.

  • 14 checklist phases from cockpit entry through securing the aircraft
  • Colour-coded per item: green when the condition is auto-detected as met, orange when detected as not met, cyan for items that can only be checked by eye
  • Conditions are evaluated from cached aircraft state on a half-second timer, not per frame
  • Automatic flight phase detection (parked, engine start, warm-up, taxi, before takeoff, airborne, approach, landed, shutdown) highlights the checklist you should be on
  • Items are variant-aware, so the Classic and Modern panels get the correct heading gyro entries
  • Landing gear items read the per-leg gear model's own truth rather than X-Plane's deploy ratio

AUTO START and AUTO SHUTDOWN Sequences NEW

  • Sequenced startup and shutdown procedures driven by a state machine that verifies each step against the aircraft's actual state before moving on, rather than snapping switches into position
  • Covers the full chain: battery on, bus voltage confirmed, inverter on, master magneto, throttle set, then the engine start sequence itself
  • Shutdown walks mixture to cutoff, waits for the engines to actually stop, then generators, inverter, magnetos and battery
  • A floating AUTO START / AUTO SHUTDOWN popup mirrors the sequence live so you can watch which step the aircraft is on. VR-aware positioning
  • Boost pumps are commanded off after a successful start, and the mixture paddle is held so the levers can physically reach cutoff

Panel State Presets ENHANCED

FAILURE SYSTEM EXPANSION

Full Failure Catalogue ENHANCED

The 2.0 failure module was two rules and a global MTBF: engine failure above 4000 prop RPM sustained for five seconds, and an engine fire if oil pressure stayed above 115 PSI for thirty seconds. It also accumulated engine "fatigue points" from low oil temperature operation. The C++ system replaces this with a catalogue of 80 discrete failure types across thirteen groups, each with its own trigger logic, symptoms and repair path.

  • Engine (14): left, right or both magnetos per engine, low oil pressure, oil leak, partial power loss, complete failure
  • Electrical (8): generator failed open, generator failed shorted and voltage regulator failure on each side, battery internal failure, inverter failure
  • Propeller (2): feather button auto-popout switch stuck, per engine
  • Circuit breakers (9): fuel boost pumps, pitot heat, autopilot, inverter, gyro horizon, directional gyro, turn and bank. Repairable in flight from the FAILURES tab of the DC3 Hangar; until then the physical breaker pops again when reset
  • Thermal relief valves (5): main hydraulic, left and right brake, gear, cowl
  • Cabin heater (8): as described in the heater section above
  • Ice protection (6): as described in the icing section above
  • Landing gear (8): actuator seal leak per side, worn downlock per side, jammed uplock per side, bungee shock cords past their two-year service life, and a sheared tail wheel lock pin, which leaves the wheel castoring freely until it is replaced
  • Flaps (4): actuator seal leak per side, relief valve stuck open, asymmetric rigging
  • Brakes (6): shuttle valve stuck and brake line leak, each per wheel, the power brake control valve, and an emergency air bottle leak
  • Vacuum (3): left and right engine-driven pump with severity encoding, plus a check valve stuck open so a dead pump's side becomes a vacuum leak
  • Compass and gyro instruments (5): KG 102A directional gyro dead or tumbled, KMT 112 / KA 51B slaving lost so the gyro free-drifts, KI 525A HSI indicator itself dead, copilot vacuum attitude gyro seized, copilot electric turn-and-bank rate gyro failed
  • Autopilot (2): KAP 140 pitch axis and roll axis monitor failures, Modern variant

Failure Triggering and Management NEW

  • Four independent trigger paths: MTBF-based random failures tied to flight hours, wear-based degradation tied to component health from the maintenance record, abuse-induced failures from exceeding CHT, oil temperature, manifold pressure or RPM limits, and manual triggers for training
  • Scheduled failures: arm a specific failure to fire immediately, at a set time after takeoff, or at a random time between 5 and 90 minutes after takeoff, for practising a known emergency
  • Severity control: failures are not all binary. Vacuum pumps, oil pressure and partial power failures carry a severity level so you get a degrading system rather than a switch
  • Cascading: one failure can trigger related failures, for example a heater thermostat stuck closed combined with a failed drop-out switch leading to a compartment fire
  • Session or persistent: a popup asks whether triggered failures should clear at the end of the session or follow the airframe. Active failures, configuration, schedules and abuse accumulators all persist to LES.ini
  • Difficulty scaling: MTBF probability and abuse thresholds are multiplied by the active difficulty preset
  • All of this is exposed in a dedicated FAILURES tab rather than the single MTBF slider of 2.0

LANDING GEAR AND FLAPS

Per-Leg Asymmetric Gear Model NEW

2.0 treated the gear as a single unit following X-Plane's deploy ratio. Each main leg is now modelled independently, so one leg can lose hydraulic holding pressure and hang partially down and unlocked while the other stays up and locked. Reconciled against T.O. 1C-47-2 paragraphs 2-348, 2-356 and 2-409 and against Quebecair emergency procedures.

  • Hydraulic actuation in both directions, with an elastic bungee shock cord balancing gear weight during travel and a cable-operated three-position mechanical safety latch holding the actuating cylinder hook
  • Gravity extension is real: latch released, valve down, no pressure, and the leg falls under its own weight plus airload. Slowing down and yawing seats the safety latch, exactly as the operator procedure describes
  • Recovery is technique dependent. Hydraulic seating with the hand pump, or a low-speed slip, both work
  • Asymmetric drag, yaw and roll from a hanging leg are applied additively through the plugin force set, so X-Plane keeps doing all of its own gear and ground physics untouched
  • An unlatched leg collapses on the ground through the gear failure seam rather than a full override
  • Per-leg visual position is published for the 3D model to animate against
  • With no gear failure active the per-leg model tracks X-Plane's normal gear exactly, so taxi and normal operation are unchanged
  • The gear warning horn now triggers off per-leg down-and-locked state, not raw deploy ratio

Gear Handle Interlock and Lock Lever NEW

  • Three-tier interlock: safety clip, lock lever, then the gear control valve handle. Each must be dealt with in order
  • The lock lever physically interlocks the gear handle, and the LOCKED / UNLOCKED indicator lights track the lock lever rather than simply mirroring deploy state
  • Premature positive lock jam modelled, a real DC-3 failure mode where moving the lock lever too early jams the mechanism
  • The lock lever can be re-latched in flight
  • Stock X-Plane gear and flap key bindings are intercepted and consumed, so the G key runs the full DC-3 procedure instead of bypassing it, and refuses gear up on the ground
  • Dedicated gear lever popup window for users without a clear view of the pedestal

Flap System Behaviour ENHANCED

  • Spring-return flap lever with a drive-window latch, so the stock 1 and 2 keys give press-and-hold continuous flap motion rather than stepping to fixed presets
  • Flap travel is gated on hydraulic pressure and slows realistically in cold, thick fluid
  • Flap failure modes wired to the hydraulic system: actuator seal leak (on Veteran an unpressurised flap creeps up under airload) and relief valve stuck open leaving the flaps inoperative
  • Gear and flap travel sounds are suppressed on aircraft reload so a reload no longer plays a phantom cycle

SURFACE DE-ICING BOOT SYSTEM

Pneumatic Boot Distributor NEW

In 2.0 the de-ice switch simply flipped X-Plane's own surface heat and boot switches. The C++ plugin models the actual installation per T.O. 1C-47-2 Section III paragraph 3-420 and Maintenance Manual Section C paragraphs 4-124 through 4-162.

  • Air supply from two engine-driven Type B-12 rotary four-vane positive-displacement pumps, one per engine, through oil separators and a pressure regulating separator
  • Regulated pressure 8 +/- 0.5 PSI, 10 PSI maximum
  • Electric distributor valve on the DEICER EQUIPMENT circuit breaker, rotating through five positions. One full revolution takes 40 seconds, each section inflating for roughly 8 seconds
  • Inflation sequence: outboard wing centre cells, outboard wing upper and lower cells, inboard wing centre cells, inboard wing upper and lower cells, then the horizontal and vertical stabiliser boots
  • Switching off does not stop the system dead. The distributor motor keeps running until the rotor reaches the exhaust port position, venting pump air overboard and deflating all boots
  • Feeds directly into the icing engine's shedding model, so inflation pressure and cycle position determine how much ice actually comes off

MODERN VARIANT AVIONICS ADDITIONS

KCS 55A Compass System, Full Component Model NEW

2.0 drew the KI 525A as a gauge reading X-Plane's heading directly. The compass system is now modelled as its four real line-replaceable units, referenced to King Installation Manual 006-00111-0010 Rev February 2002 and the KCS 55A Pilot's Guide.

  • KMT 112 magnetic azimuth transmitter (flux valve): wingtip-mounted flux gate sensing the earth's field. Models bank-induced dip error, where a banked wing tilts the valve out of the local horizontal plane and it picks up the vertical component of the field, with error scaling by bank angle and magnetic dip. Optional installer deviation correction. Signal is lost when the KG 102A is unpowered
  • KG 102A directional gyro: the core of the system, hosting the slaving circuitry and powering the rest of it. Slaving sensitivity +/- 0.5 degrees, slow slave rate 3 degrees per minute, fast slave 180 degrees per minute. Unlike the older KCS 55 it fast-slaves until the error is actually zero rather than stopping on a timer. Tumble detection triggers auto fast-slave above 25 degrees per second of yaw rate. Warm-up runs from 1 minute at +55 C to 5 minutes at -46 C, interpolated, and the system-valid signal that the HSI and RMI gate on only appears once warm. Draws 1.5 A at 28 V DC
  • KA 51B slaving control: panel-mounted head with a live slaving meter showing the heading error between flux valve and gyro, a mechanical push-on / push-off SLAVE latching button that springs back out when freed, and CW / CCW manual slew buttons for free-gyro mode. The meter dies with power and the system reverts to free gyro

Glideslope pointers come into view on a delay after a usable signal appears, per the installation manual. Underdamped spring motion on the course disc, eased CDI deviation bar, accelerating HDG and CRS knobs with 0.1 degree fine steps that ramp with turn speed, and knob meshes geared 4:1 against the bug they drive
  • All components share the G2 COMPASS circuit breaker

Referenced to KI 229 Installation Manual IM 006-00192-0002 Rev 2, January 1983. Without this module the 3D model would read X-Plane's raw values and work regardless of power.

INSTRUMENTS AND COCKPIT ANIMATION

Dynamic Aircraft Registration NEW

2.0 read the tail number from a data file and wrote it to X-Plane's registration string. It never appeared on the airframe. The C++ plugin renders it onto the aircraft.

  • Tail number drawn with Cairo and FreeType, composited onto the 4096 x 4096 panel texture through a GLSL shader, with the exterior meshes referencing the panel texture
  • Three draw regions taken from the registration object's UV bounds: right rear fuselage, left rear fuselage and left wing underside, the last shifted down to follow the taper of the mesh
  • Up to eight characters, also exposed for external tool compatibility
  • Live source in the DISPATCH tab, so changing the registration updates the airframe without a reload

Tumbler Drum Animation System NEW

  • Generic rolling-digit drum system used by the radio frequency and DME counters, and available for any other mechanical digit stack
  • Handles the mechanical "hold and snap" carry-over between drums automatically, so each drum in the 3D model only needs a simple ten-step rotation instead of a hand-built multi-keyframe animation

Altimeter and Airspeed Refinements ENHANCED

  • Direct-drive, smoothed indicated-altitude needles with a matching Kollsman baro disc on the Modern panel
  • Clamped baro range for the Kollsman disc and setting knob (28.00 to 31.00 inHg)
  • Classic MPH airspeed needle reads the failure-aware pilot gauge, so a pitot failure shows on the needle

Gyro Cage Mechanics NEW

  • Directional gyro cage knob modelled as the real push-and-rotate combination: caging the gyro is what allows the heading to be set, and the caged card follows the knob
  • Cage cam spring decay and plunger physics, so the knob behaves mechanically rather than as a toggle
  • Panel DG tumble, and automatic caging of the Sperry Gyro Turn Unit on low spin
  • Unified drift model shared between the panel DG and the autopilot's own directional gyro
  • Rudder twitch on caging the autopilot gyro eliminated

Modern Panel Gauge Behaviour NEW

  • Turbulence-driven needle jitter on the Modern airspeed indicators, pilot and copilot, derived from X-Plane's turbulence, gust, wind and G-load rather than a fixed wobble
  • Bendix/King attitude indicator degrading with the plugin's own vacuum model
  • Copilot attitude indicator runs on its own independent vacuum gyro with its own failure
  • Copilot turn-and-bank runs on an electric rate gyro with spool-up, circuit breaker gating and its own failure
  • Turn needle clamped to the +/- 6 degree keyframe range of the physical instrument
  • Gyro readiness bar in the STATUS tab covering both the vacuum AI and the electric HSI spin-up

Propeller Disc Rendering NEW

  • Uses X-Plane's custom prop disc system to replace the default propeller rendering with a texture-atlas billboard system built for the three-blade Hamilton Standard Hydromatic
  • Physical 3D blades are shown at low RPM; above roughly 563 prop RPM (about 1000 engine RPM) the plugin switches to front disc and side billboard rendering with motion-blur textures, with hysteresis to prevent flickering at the transition
  • Four-cell atlas shared by both engines: three increasing blur levels for the front disc and one cell of side blade profiles, one row per engine

Cockpit Hat Pendulum Physics NEW

  • The hat hanging in the cockpit swings as a real pendulum driven by aircraft accelerations, published as X and Y swing angles
  • The two hats have slightly different pendulum length and damping, so they drift out of phase under any sustained excitation and produce organic, asynchronous motion rather than a synchronised wobble

Particle and Visual Effects ENHANCED

  • Engine exhaust flame intensity, propeller tip condensation driven by actual humidity and prop speed, wingtip vortices and engine exhaust smoke, all driving X-Plane 12's particle system

SYSTEM DIAGNOSTICS AND MAINTENANCE

Maintenance and Wear ENHANCED

2.0 offered oil changes, spark plugs, engine overhaul, fluid fill-ups and battery recharge as buttons with a condition percentage. The C++ system keeps all of that and ties it into the failure and difficulty systems.

  • Component health drives wear-based failure probability directly, scaled by the difficulty mode
  • Abuse accumulators track time spent over CHT, oil temperature, manifold pressure and RPM limits and persist between sessions, so mistreating the engines has consequences later
  • Service interval tracking, per-leak repair and per-tank or per-engine servicing
  • The MAINTENANCE tab's Condition block showed two engines and nothing else. It now carries an Airframe Systems row as well, with bars for the hydraulic pumps, the landing gear, the flaps and the brakes on the same colour thresholds. The first three had been scored all along and never shown, so a hydraulic system could be flown most of the way down with no number anywhere to say so
  • Everything persists through LES.ini alongside failure state and configuration

State Persistence ENHANCED

  • Sectioned INI persistence covering the persistent panel state, difficulty, active failures, failure configuration, failure schedules and abuse accumulators
  • Atomic save, so an interrupted write cannot corrupt the file
  • Each system writes only its own section rather than rewriting the whole file, so parallel systems cannot clobber each other
  • Coverage extended over the development cycle to include console controls, flood lights, audio settings, the oxygen valve, cowl knobs, the friction lock, the hand pump shutoff valve, the de-ice boot master switch, firewall shutoff valve positions and the per-zone icing state

SOUND

2.0 exposed a small set of FMOD values from LES_32.lua. The C++ plugin publishes a comprehensive, purpose-built interface for the sound bank.

  • Systems: hydraulic pump and hand pump, wiper hydraulic hiss, per-engine CO2 discharge, fire warning bell, gear warning horn, clock, reading lights
  • Environmental: wind with airspeed and weather gating, airframe creaks and rattles in light and heavy chop and on firm touchdowns

  • Radio audio is generated as PCM by the plugin, including static, range station tones and the Z-marker, and handed to FMOD

ADDITIONAL FIXES AND CORRECTIONS

Engine and Fuel

  • Firewall shutoff valves are now opened by the start procedures. Closed valves were the real reason engines would sometimes refuse to start
  • Engine start stops pulsing the ignition once the engine is lit, and no longer keeps feeding a dying engine
  • The starter assist is carried through to idle, and the engine is verified running before the state machine declares it so
  • Flameout detection while running
  • Idle RPM reconciled across the plugin and both aircraft files at the installed figure of 800 RPM, rather than the R-1830 manual's test-stand figure for an engine driving nothing
  • The direct-crank starter was removed. Both variants use the Eclipse E-80 inertia starter, which is what the aircraft actually has
  • Generator cut-in RPM made variant-specific: 950 on the Classic, 1300 on the Modern
  • Primer switches work correctly in Veteran mode, where there is no auto-cutoff
  • Fuel quantity selector wrapping fixed, and its detented behaviour documented
  • Fuel quantity now also reported in US gallons for the Modern gauge

Electrical

  • Peukert's Law implemented in battery discharge, so high current draw genuinely reduces available capacity
  • Battery cold-capacity model corrected and wired to actual depletion
  • Inverted battery voltage sign fixed
  • Stale discharge current reading fixed
  • Avionics power is published correctly on a shared cockpit client, so the joining pilot's gauges are not dead

Hydraulics and Fire Protection

  • Dedicated gear-circuit pressure gauge with seal-failure bleed-down
  • The hand pump warms the fluid directly, which is the intended cold-weather technique
  • Thermal relief valves actually relieve pressure, and latched damage is no longer re-applied every frame
  • The engine fire state was repaired, which revived the entire fire chain from detection through warning to suppression
  • Nacelle engine fires now extinguish on a proper CO2 ramp rather than instantly
  • The heater compartment fire can be both started and extinguished
  • Fire warning bell on a real fire, a heater fire lamp, and the correct behaviour for a dead engine

Icing and Anti-Ice

  • Propeller alcohol tank re-specified to 50 litres, with a matching gauge needle on the Modern panel
  • Carburettor heat thermodynamics reworked
  • The never-applied pitching-moment icing penalty was removed rather than left as dead weight

Shared Cockpit

  • Authoritative state mirror on the client, with input suppression so both machines cannot fight over the same control
  • Connect-time state synchronisation, so a pilot joining mid-flight inherits the current aircraft state rather than a default one
  • Authentication hardening
  • Modern avionics control forwarding, so radio and autopilot manipulation is mirrored to the other pilot
  • Mirrored state refreshed for the gauges the client actually reads
  • A circuit breaker pulled or reset by the joining pilot reaches the host, which replays it so its own panel pops, clicks and animates, and the host's state comes back (protocol v5: both machines need the same build)
  • The joining pilot's carburettor air temperature gauge and carburettor icing caution follow the host's

Housekeeping

  • The RealityXP GPS options were removed. The integration had never been functional end to end, and leaving inert options in the interface is worse than not offering them
  • Credits consolidated, with Turbine Sound Studios added
